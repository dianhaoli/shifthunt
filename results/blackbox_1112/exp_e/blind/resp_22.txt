# Using a sparse autoencoder (SAE) to remove spurious reliance

This follows the approach in work like SHIFT (Marks et al., *Sparse Feature Circuits*). The idea is to decompose the classifier's internal activations into interpretable features, find the ones that encode the spurious cue, remove them, and check that the classifier still works.

## Setup
Suppose you have a classifier `f = head(g(x))`, where `g` is the backbone (e.g., a transformer) and `h = g_l(x)` is the activation at layer `l`. Suppose it relies on a spurious attribute `s` (e.g., background, gender pronouns, watermark) that correlates with the label `y` in training data.

## Step 1: Choose a layer and collect activations
- Pick a layer where the spurious concept is linearly represented, often a mid-to-late layer or the penultimate layer. You can check this by training a probe for `s` at several layers.
- Collect activations over a large, diverse dataset: the training distribution, ideally plus some unlabeled or out-of-distribution data so the dictionary isn't specific to the shortcut.

## Step 2: Train the SAE
- Architecture: `z = ReLU(W_enc (h − b_dec) + b_enc)`, `ĥ = W_dec z + b_dec`. Variants such as TopK or JumpReLU also work.
- Loss: `‖h − ĥ‖² + λ‖z‖₁` (or a TopK constraint instead of the L1 term).
- Use an overcomplete dictionary (e.g., 8–64× the width of `h`) and normalize the decoder columns.
- Check reconstruction quality by splicing `ĥ` back into the model and measuring the change in classifier loss or accuracy. If the splice hurts accuracy badly, the SAE isn't good enough for intervention.

## Step 3: Identify candidate spurious latents
Use several complementary signals:
1. **Correlation with `s`:** compute each latent's mean activation or AUROC for predicting `s`, using examples labeled for the spurious attribute (this can be a small set).
2. **Attribution to the classifier:** compute gradient × activation, or integrated gradients, of the classifier's logit with respect to each latent `z_i`. This shows which latents the classifier actually uses.
3. **Inspection:** look at top-activating examples and, optionally, auto-generated descriptions. Flag latents that represent the spurious concept and not the true task signal.

Latents that score high on both attribution and `s`-correlation are the main candidates. Human review (or an LLM judge) can serve as the final filter, since that's the point of having interpretable features.

## Step 4: Validate causally
Before trusting the candidates, intervene and see what happens:
- Ablate the candidate latents (set `z_i = 0`, or mean-ablate), then decode and pass `ĥ'` to the rest of the network.
- Check that the spurious probe's accuracy drops, that the classifier's dependence on `s` drops (e.g., predictions stop changing when you flip `s` in counterfactual inputs), and that accuracy on the true task is preserved.
- If ablation hurts core-task accuracy, some selected latents carry the real signal or are entangled with it. Refine the set.

## Step 5: Apply the edit
Preserve the SAE's reconstruction error so you don't damage everything else:

```
z      = SAE.encode(h)
err    = h - SAE.decode(z)
z'     = z.clone(); z'[:, S] = 0       # S = spurious latent set
h_edit = SAE.decode(z') + err
```

Alternatively, project out the decoder directions `W_dec[S]` from `h` directly, which doesn't require running the SAE at inference time.

## Step 6: Make the removal stick by retraining
Ablation alone often leaves residual reliance, because the spurious signal may be spread across other latents or in the error term. The more robust option is to:
- Apply the edit to the activations for the training set.
- Retrain (or fine-tune) the classifier head on the edited activations, optionally with the edit active during fine-tuning of later layers.

Because the spurious feature is no longer available, the head is pushed to rely on the remaining, genuine features. This retraining step is what SHIFT does.

## Step 7: Evaluate
- **Worst-group accuracy** on groups where `s` and `y` disagree (e.g., Waterbirds, CelebA, Bias in Bios).
- **Spurious probe accuracy** after the edit (should fall toward chance).
- **Average accuracy**, to confirm no major loss.
- **Counterfactual or flipped-`s` tests**, to confirm predictions are invariant to the spurious cue.
- Use held-out data, not the data used to select the latents, to avoid overfitting your intervention.

## Caveats
- **Feature splitting and absorption:** the spurious concept may be spread across many latents, or merged into other latents. Try different dictionary sizes and check for leftover signal using a fresh probe.
- **Residual leakage:** the SAE error term and unselected latents may still encode `s`. Always retrain a probe after the edit to measure remaining information.
- **Entanglement:** if `s` and the true feature are causally or semantically tied, removal may hurt performance.
- **Selection requires some supervision:** you need some labels for `s`, or human judgment, to pick latents.
- **Distribution shift:** an SAE trained on one distribution may not represent features well on another.
- **Alternatives for comparison:** linear concept erasure (INLP, LEACE) and group-robust training (Group DRO). An SAE is most useful when you want interpretable, inspectable control over what is removed.
