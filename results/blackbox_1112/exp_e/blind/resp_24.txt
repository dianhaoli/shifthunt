A sparse autoencoder (SAE) can help identify and intervene on internal features associated with a spurious cue—for example, a classifier using snow to predict “wolf.”

1. **Collect activations.** Run a representative dataset through the classifier and record activations from a chosen layer. Include examples that break the usual correlation, such as wolves without snow and dogs in snow, if available.

2. **Train an SAE.** Freeze the classifier and train an encoder–decoder on those activations. The SAE learns to reconstruct each activation using a small number of active latent features, encouraged by a sparsity penalty.

3. **Identify candidate spurious features.** Look for SAE latents that activate strongly on examples containing the cue. Use metadata, example inspection, or probes to check whether they track the cue rather than just the class label.

4. **Test whether the features matter causally.** Intervene on candidate latents and measure the change in predictions. For instance, reduce a snow-associated latent on wolf images and see whether the wolf score drops. Compare against controls and counterfactual examples; correlation alone is not enough to establish reliance.

5. **Intervene in the classifier.** At inference time, modify the activation before passing it to the remaining layers. A simple intervention subtracts the candidate feature’s decoded contribution:
   \[
   h' = h - z_j d_j,
   \]
   where \(z_j\) is the SAE activation for feature \(j\) and \(d_j\) is its decoder direction. Alternatively, replace \(z_j\) with a baseline value rather than zero. Multiple latents may need intervention if the cue is distributed.

6. **Evaluate and refine.** Test on held-out examples, especially groups where the cue and label disagree. Measure worst-group and overall accuracy, check for damage to legitimate predictions, and compare with unmodified and control interventions. If needed, use the identified features to guide retraining or regularization.

This is an empirical mitigation, not a guarantee: SAE features can be entangled, and the classifier may encode the same spurious cue elsewhere.
