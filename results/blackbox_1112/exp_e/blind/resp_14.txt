I’d treat probe accuracy as a starting point, not evidence that the probe has isolated the intended concept. It might instead use correlated cues such as topic, token identity, position, syntax, or text length.

**Check what it relies on:**

1. **Test on matched and counterfactual examples.** Change the concept while holding likely nuisance features fixed, and vice versa. For example, swap a concept-bearing word while preserving sentence structure, or change the topic without changing the concept. The probe should respond to the former but remain stable under the latter.
2. **Evaluate under distribution shift.** Hold out entire templates, topics, authors, or lexical items—not just random examples. Compare with simple baselines that use suspected shortcuts directly.
3. **Run controls.** Check performance on shuffled labels and test whether the same activations or probe scores predict nuisance variables. Inspect high-confidence errors for systematic patterns.
4. **If the claim is that the model *uses* the concept, intervene.** Modify the putative concept direction in the activation and measure the effect on model behavior, comparing with matched random or irrelevant directions. Interpret this cautiously: activation edits can push the model off its normal activation distribution.

**Fix it:** Rebuild or augment the training data so the concept is balanced across nuisance features, include hard negatives and counterfactual pairs, and use grouped splits. If necessary, explicitly penalize nuisance predictability or remove known nuisance directions, then retrain. Finally, repeat the same held-out and intervention tests; a fix that only improves the original test set may just introduce a new shortcut.

Even a robust probe shows that information is *decodable* from activations. Establishing that the model uses it requires separate causal evidence.
