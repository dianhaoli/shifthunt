# Using a Sparse Autoencoder to Remove Spurious Reliance

The best-known version of this approach is **SHIFT** (Sparse Human-Interpretable Feature Trimming), from Marks et al., *Sparse Feature Circuits* (2024). The idea is to decompose the classifier's internal activations into interpretable features, find the features carrying the spurious signal, and ablate them. Its main advantage is that **you don't need data where the spurious feature and the label come apart**. Human judgment about which features are illegitimate substitutes for that data.

Their running example is a profession classifier (professor vs. nurse) trained on Bias in Bios. In the training data, gender is perfectly correlated with profession, so the classifier learns to rely on gender.

---

## Step 1: Set up the model and the problem

- Start with a classifier, typically a pretrained LM with a linear probe or classification head trained on your task.
- Assume the training data contains a spurious correlation, e.g. all professors are male and all nurses are female.
- Keep a small **held-out test set where the correlation is broken** (balanced or anti-correlated). Use it only for evaluation, not for choosing interventions.

## Step 2: Obtain sparse autoencoders for the model's activations

Train or reuse SAEs at the relevant locations: residual stream, attention outputs, and MLP outputs at several layers. Each SAE decomposes an activation vector into sparse features:

$$x \approx \sum_i f_i(x)\, d_i + b + \varepsilon(x)$$

- $f_i(x)$ are sparse, nonnegative feature activations.
- $d_i$ are decoder directions.
- $\varepsilon(x)$ is the **reconstruction error**. Keep it explicitly in the computation so that inserting SAEs doesn't change the model's outputs.

The SAEs are trained on generic data, not your task data. This matters because their features should reflect the model's general representations rather than your biased dataset.

## Step 3: Find the features that matter for the classifier's decision

Not all features matter, and there are too many to inspect, so score each feature by its causal effect on the classifier output. Let the metric $m$ be the logit difference between classes. Then:

- **Attribution patching (linear approximation):**
  $$\text{IE}(f_i) \approx \nabla_{f_i} m \cdot \big(0 - f_i(x)\big)$$
  This estimates the effect of zero-ablating feature $i$.
- **Integrated gradients** is more accurate for early layers or saturating nonlinearities.
- Average the scores over task inputs, then keep features above a threshold. Score the error terms too, so you know how much is left unexplained.

The result is a **sparse feature circuit**: a manageable set of features (tens to a few hundred) that drive the classifier.

## Step 4: Interpret the features

For each important feature, look at:

- **Max-activating examples** from a large corpus, and which tokens fire it.
- **Output effects**: which logits its decoder direction promotes or suppresses.
- Optionally, automated interpretation (an LLM-generated explanation), checked by a human.

## Step 5: Judge which features are task-irrelevant

This is the human-in-the-loop step. Mark features as spurious if they encode information that *shouldn't* matter for the task. In the gender example:

- **Spurious:** features firing on "he/she/his/her," female names, "woman," "mother," gendered titles.
- **Legitimate:** features for "nursing," "hospital," "patients," "university," "research," "professor."

The judgment depends on what the feature *means*, not on its statistics in your dataset. That is why no disambiguating data is required.

## Step 6: Ablate the spurious features

Intervene during the forward pass:

- **Zero-ablate** the selected features (set $f_i = 0$), or mean-ablate them using a mean from general data.
- Equivalently, subtract $f_i(x)\, d_i$ from the activation at that location.
- Keep the error term $\varepsilon(x)$ untouched, so everything you didn't explicitly remove is preserved.

Apply the ablation at all relevant layers. Gender information may be re-derivable downstream if you only remove it in one place.

## Step 7 (optional): Retrain the classifier head with ablations in place

The ablated model's head was fit under the old representation, so performance may drop. Retrain the head, or fine-tune lightly, on the **original biased training data** with the ablations active. This is SHIFT + retrain. Because the spurious features are gone, the head can't re-learn the shortcut from them and must rely on what remains.

## Step 8: Evaluate

On the balanced held-out set, measure:

- **Profession accuracy.** Should rise substantially. In the paper it went from ~61% to ~88–93%.
- **Gender accuracy** (how well the classifier's outputs predict gender). Should fall toward chance.
- **Worst-group accuracy**, e.g. female professors and male nurses.

Compare against two reference points:
- An **oracle** classifier trained on balanced data (an upper bound).
- Baselines such as concept bleaching or random feature ablation.

Also confirm that rerunning attribution after ablation shows no surviving gender-related features.

---

## Pitfalls and caveats

- **Reconstruction error.** Spurious information can hide in $\varepsilon(x)$, which you can't interpret or selectively ablate. Check how much of the effect flows through error nodes.
- **Entangled features.** A feature like "nurse" may partly encode gender because the two co-occur in the world. Removing only clearly gendered features leaves some leakage. Removing too aggressively hurts the task.
- **Interpretation errors.** A feature labeled from its top activations may behave differently at lower activation levels. Spot-check across the activation range.
- **Redundancy.** Models often encode the same information in several places, so check multiple layers.
- **Human judgment is the bottleneck.** It scales poorly with feature count and depends on the analyst noticing the bias. You must already suspect, or discover from the features, what is spurious.
- **SAE quality limits everything.** Poorly trained SAEs with dense, polysemantic, or split features make clean removal impossible.

## Related alternatives

- **Linear concept erasure** (INLP, LEACE) removes a concept's linear subspace, but needs labels for the spurious attribute.
- **Group-robust training** (DRO, reweighting) needs group labels or data where the correlation breaks.
- **Projecting out a single SAE decoder direction** is a simpler variant when one feature dominates.

The distinctive advantage of the SAE approach is that interpretability replaces labeled counterexamples. The main cost is reliance on SAE quality and on human review.
