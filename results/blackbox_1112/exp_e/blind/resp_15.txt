# Checking and Fixing a Linear Probe That Learned the Wrong Thing

High probe accuracy shows that *some* linearly decodable signal correlates with your labels. It doesn't show that the signal is your concept. Common culprits include sentence length, specific tokens or lexical cues, position, topic, formatting, and dataset construction artifacts. For example, all your "true" statements might come from one template.

## Part 1: Diagnosing the problem

### 1. Start with baselines
- **Input-level baselines:** Train the same probe on bag-of-words features, token embeddings (layer 0), or simple features like length. If these do nearly as well, your labels are recoverable from surface features.
- **Random/untrained model:** Probe a randomly initialized copy of the model. High accuracy here means the probe is doing the work, not the representation.
- **Control tasks (Hewitt & Liang, 2019):** Assign random but consistent labels to word types and train the probe on them. A probe that fits these well is expressive enough to memorize. Report *selectivity*, the gap between real-task and control-task accuracy.

### 2. Break the correlation in your test data
Build evaluation sets in which the concept and the suspected confound vary independently:
- **Minimal pairs / counterfactuals:** Change only the concept and keep surface form fixed ("The capital of France is Paris" vs. "...is Rome"). Then do the reverse: change surface form and keep the concept fixed (paraphrases, different templates, different lengths).
- **Stratified evaluation:** Within each bin of the confound (same length, same template, same topic), check whether the probe still works.
- **Out-of-distribution transfer:** Train on one dataset or domain and test on another constructed differently. Spurious features usually fail to transfer; the real concept should.
- **Anti-correlated sets:** Deliberately flip the confound's usual relationship to the label and see whether accuracy collapses or inverts.

### 3. Probe the confound directly
- Train a separate probe for each suspected confound (length, sentiment, a specific token's presence).
- Compute the cosine similarity between that probe's direction and your concept direction. High similarity is a red flag.
- See whether the concept probe's scores predict the confound on data where the concept doesn't apply.

### 4. Look at what the probe responds to
- Examine the highest- and lowest-scoring examples from a large, diverse corpus, not just your labeled set. If the top examples share a surface pattern rather than the concept, you've found your confound.
- Run the probe on text where the concept is absent or undefined and see what it fires on.
- Compare with a difference-of-means direction. If a logistic regression probe and the class-mean difference point in very different directions, the logistic probe may be exploiting low-variance quirks.

### 5. Test causally
Decoding is correlational. Intervention tells you whether the model *uses* the direction:
- **Ablate or project out** the probe direction and check whether behavior that depends on the concept degrades, while unrelated behavior stays intact.
- **Steer** along the direction and check whether outputs shift in the concept-relevant way (e.g., the model starts treating false statements as true) rather than changing style, length, or topic.
- **Patch activations** between minimal-pair inputs and see whether the concept-relevant output follows.

If a confound probe's direction produces the same causal effects, you haven't separated the two.

## Part 2: Fixing it

### Fix the data first
- **Decorrelate:** Balance the training set so the confound is equally distributed across labels. Match lengths, use multiple templates per class, and mix topics.
- **Counterfactual augmentation:** Add minimal pairs so the only consistent signal separating classes is the concept.
- **Contrastive training:** Train on *differences* between paired activations (same prompt, concept flipped). Shared surface features cancel out.
- **Diversify sources:** Train on several datasets built in different ways, so no single artifact is consistently predictive.

### Remove known confounds from the representation
- **Concept erasure:** Use LEACE or iterative nullspace projection (INLP) to remove linearly decodable information about the confound, then retrain the concept probe on the cleaned activations.
- **Residualize:** Regress activations (or probe scores) on confound features and probe the residuals.
- **Adversarial training:** Penalize the probe if its output or direction is predictive of the confound.

### Constrain the probe
- Use strong regularization, or prefer difference-of-means / LDA-style directions, which are less prone to exploiting tiny high-weight features.
- Check stability: retrain with different seeds and data splits. A real concept direction should be consistent; a spurious one often wanders.
- Try several layers. A concept that appears only at an implausible layer, or is equally decodable everywhere including layer 0, is suspicious.

### Re-validate
After any fix, rerun the diagnostics: OOD transfer, stratified accuracy, selectivity against controls, and above all the causal interventions. A fix only counts if the probe holds up on data designed to break it.

## A caveat
Sometimes the problem isn't the probe. The model may not represent your concept separately from the confound, at least not linearly at that layer. If no amount of decorrelation produces a direction that generalizes and works causally, the honest conclusion may be that the model has entangled the two, or that it tracks a proxy rather than your concept. That's a substantive finding, not a probing failure.
