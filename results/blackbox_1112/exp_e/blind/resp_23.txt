Here is a step-by-step method for using a sparse autoencoder (SAE) to remove a classifier's reliance on a spurious feature.

**Setup and assumptions**
- You have a trained classifier `f = head ∘ g`, where `g` maps inputs to an intermediate representation `h` at some layer.
- You have a dataset with class labels `y` and a spurious attribute `s` (e.g., "watermark present," "background = water"). Ideally you also have a held-out set where `s` is decorrelated from `y`, so you can measure reliance on it. You don't need `s` labels for training the SAE, but you need them to identify and validate the spurious latents.

**Step 1: Collect activations**
Run a diverse dataset through `g` and record `h` at the chosen layer. Include examples where `s` and `y` are decorrelated, since the SAE should see the spurious feature varying independently of the label.

**Step 2: Train the SAE**
Train an encoder and decoder on the activations:
- `z = ReLU(W_enc h + b_enc)` (or TopK/JumpReLU for sparsity control)
- `ĥ = W_dec z + b_dec`
- Loss: `||h − ĥ||²` plus a sparsity penalty such as `λ||z||₁`.

Check reconstruction quality, the fraction of dead latents, and sparsity levels before continuing. A poor SAE will make the later steps unreliable.

**Step 3: Find candidate spurious latents**
Score each latent `z_i` on two criteria:
- *Association with the spurious attribute:* measure how well `z_i` predicts `s`, e.g., AUROC or mutual information, while controlling for `y` (for example, compare activations for `s=1` vs `s=0` within each class).
- *Causal relevance to the classifier:* compute attribution of the logit (or loss) to each latent, e.g., gradient × activation, `a_i = (∂L/∂z_i) · z_i`, or the direct effect of the latent through the head `w_y^T W_dec[:, i] z_i`.

Latents that score high on both are the spurious candidates. You can also inspect top-activating examples or use automated interpretation to confirm that a latent corresponds to the spurious concept rather than the class.

**Step 4: Disentangle from core features**
If a latent correlates with both `s` and `y`, it is likely a core feature. Remove or down-weight any latent that is strongly tied to the class. Verify on counterfactual or balanced examples where possible.

**Step 5: Intervene on the representation**
Edit the activations by removing only the spurious component:
- Encode: `z = SAE_enc(h)`
- Zero the spurious latents: `z'_i = 0` for `i ∈ S` (or replace with their mean activation)
- Reconstruct while preserving the residual:
  `h' = W_dec z' + b_dec + (h − ĥ)`

Keeping the reconstruction error `(h − ĥ)` matters. Otherwise you discard everything the SAE failed to capture, which may include core information. Alternatively, project out the spurious decoder directions: `h' = h − Σ_{i∈S} z_i d_i`.

**Step 6: Retrain or recalibrate the head**
Removing the features may not eliminate all reliance, since the head's weights may still read the spurious direction or the signal may be distributed. Options:
- Retrain the final linear layer on `h'` using group-balanced data (e.g., group DRO or reweighting by `(y, s)` groups).
- Or fine-tune the upper layers with the SAE-based edit inserted in the forward pass, so the model learns to rely on the remaining features.

**Step 7: Evaluate**
- *Worst-group accuracy* and accuracy on the decorrelated test set should improve.
- *Spurious probe test:* train a linear probe for `s` on `h'`. Its accuracy should drop toward chance. If it doesn't, the information is distributed beyond the selected latents.
- *Residual check:* probe the SAE error term `(h − ĥ)` for `s`. If it still predicts `s`, the spurious information is outside the SAE's features, and you may need a larger dictionary or a different layer.
- *Core accuracy:* confirm in-distribution accuracy hasn't dropped significantly.
- *Sanity check:* ablate random latents of similar activation frequency as a control.

**Step 8: Iterate**
Vary the number of ablated latents, the layer, and the SAE size. Watch for "hydra" effects, where the model routes the spurious information through other latents after ablation. Re-run Step 3 on the edited model if needed.

**Caveats**
- SAE latents are sometimes polysemantic, so ablating one may remove useful information too.
- If `s` is causally entangled with `y` (e.g., it's a partial feature of the true class), removing it can cost core accuracy.
- Simpler baselines, such as linear concept erasure (e.g., LEACE) or training a probe for `s` and projecting its direction out, often work as well. The SAE's main advantage is that its latents are interpretable and can be checked by hand.
